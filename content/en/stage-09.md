# Epilogue — The Machine at the Door

**Hook**: AI did not solve the Riemann hypothesis. So why will mathematicians remember 2026 as a special year?

In the summer of 2026, an Anthropic staff member made a rather bold request to an unreleased version of Claude: "Take a real stab at the Riemann hypothesis." No mathematical choices were made for it by people.

Claude did not prove the Riemann hypothesis, and Anthropic said so. Instead, in the race we saw in Stage 7 — the "guaranteed proportion of zeros on the line" — it got past a wall that had stood for a long time, raising that lower bound from 41.6% to 67.2%.

What came next matters. Two of the company's mathematicians studied the argument, and Conrey and Goldston, senior experts in the field, examined it too. And the proof was formalized in Lean, a proof-checking program, and passed machine verification.

Two months later, in October, OpenAI announced a proof of a "quasi-Riemann hypothesis". It does not say the zeros lie on one line, but that they are trapped in a band narrower than width 1. The first version claimed real parts between 1/8 and 7/8 (verified in Lean); a shorter version about a week later claimed 1/12 to 11/12. One mathematician remarked that "the vast majority of consequences of RH already follow from quasi-RH".

[graphic:ai-results] The two results laid over the zeta landscape — a gauge for "proportion on the line" and a "narrowed band". One line remains out of reach.

Mathematicians immediately began taking the paper apart. One called it "the most significant AI-for-math achievement so far". Another pointed out it was written so badly a journal would reject it on sight. Still, short human write-ups began to circulate, and experts largely believe the result is correct. Of Claude's result, meanwhile, it was said that there is no visible path from its methods to the real Riemann hypothesis — and Anthropic itself takes the same view.

So the true meaning of these events lies in the method. AI finds a path, people read and refine it, machines check it. Both results surprised people by bringing no new concepts, but weaving together existing ideas in ways no one had thought of.

And we return to the sound from the prologue. Now you know what it is: the music of the primes, played by countless zeros. And the Riemann hypothesis is one sentence: "every instrument sounds at the same volume."

Who will prove that sentence at last — a person, a machine, or the two together?

**Junior line**: "AI didn't beat the final boss, but it beat a mid-boss nobody had beaten in 50 years. And people and computers double-checked the record."
